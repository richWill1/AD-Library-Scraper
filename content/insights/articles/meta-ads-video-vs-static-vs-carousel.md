"Should we be doing more video?" comes up in almost every Meta account review. The honest answer is that it depends on what the ad needs to do, who it is for and where it will be seen. Meta's delivery system decides, impression by impression, which of your ads to show to whom. Your job is to give it formats that each do a clear job, and then to measure them fairly.

This guide covers what each of the three core formats does well, where each one struggles, how to design a format test that tells you something, and how to benchmark your creative mix against competitors without misreading the data.

:::takeaways
- No format wins universally. Video, static and carousel suit different messages, products and placements.
- Format tests are only useful if the message, offer and audience stay constant. Otherwise you are testing the idea, not the format.
- Judge formats on the outcome you are buying, such as cost per purchase or lead, not on click-through rate alone.
- Competitors' creative mix is a useful benchmark, but ad counts are not spend. Use shares and look at which formats they keep iterating.
:::

## Why there is no universal winner

You will find case studies claiming video outperforms static by large margins, and others claiming the opposite. Both can be true for the advertiser who ran the test. Results depend on:

- **The product.** Products that need demonstrating, such as gadgets, skincare routines or software, benefit from motion. Products that sell on a single clear visual, such as a print, a pair of trainers or a sofa in a room, often do not need it.
- **The message.** A strong offer ("30% off this weekend") can be delivered in a static image in under a second. A story ("why we started making this") needs time.
- **The placement.** Reels and Stories are full-screen and vertical, and built for motion. Feed and right-column placements reward instant clarity.
- **Production quality.** A well-made static usually beats a poorly made video, and vice versa.

Meta's delivery also adds a layer. Meta's engineering team has described a retrieval system, Andromeda, that narrows tens of millions of candidate ads to a few thousand before ranking them for each person. Practitioners such as Jon Loomer interpret this as a reason to give the system genuinely different creative to choose from, rather than many near-identical variants. That is a reasonable reading, but it is interpretation rather than a published Meta rule.

## Video: strengths and limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Shows the product working, in context | More expensive and slower to produce and iterate |
| Carries a narrative, founder story or explanation | Many viewers scroll past before the key message |
| Native to Reels and Stories, which are full-screen and vertical | Hard to read with the sound off unless captioned |
| Can combine demonstration, proof and offer in one asset | A weak opening wastes the rest of the asset |

**What tends to matter most:** the opening seconds, because that is where the scroll is won or lost; captions or on-screen text, because many people watch with the sound off; and vertical framing for Reels and Stories placements. Put the product and the core benefit on screen early rather than building up to them.

**What to test in video:** different openings on the same body footage, user-generated against studio production, short cuts against longer explainers, and talking-head against product-only.

## Static images: strengths and limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Fast and cheap to produce, so you can test many angles | Limited room to explain anything complex |
| Instant clarity, ideal for a single offer or proposition | Can look like generic advertising if over-designed |
| Easy to adapt for different placements and messages | Less native in Reels than vertical video |
| Text-led statics can carry strong copy-led angles | Often fatigues faster when the message is the only variable |

**What tends to matter most:** one clear idea per image, legible at thumbnail size on a phone, and a visual that stops the scroll without needing the caption.

**What to test in static:** product shot against lifestyle, text-led ("the problem, stated plainly") against image-led, review or testimonial cards, and before-and-after where claims can be substantiated.

## Carousels: strengths and limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Show several products, or several benefits of one product | Most people never swipe past the first card or two |
| Step-by-step storytelling: problem, solution, proof, offer | Card order and the first card carry most of the weight |
| Can be catalogue-powered, showing products relevant to each person | Catalogue quality (images, titles, prices) limits the result |
| Card-level links let you send people to specific products | Harder to brief and design than a single asset |

Catalogue-powered formats, now marketed as Advantage+ catalogue ads, deserve a mention here. They require a product catalogue connected to your pixel or Conversions API events, and they are often strongest for retargeting product viewers and for large ranges. Their creative is largely your product feed, so improving feed images and titles is a creative task.

**What to test in carousel:** the first card (a hook card against a product card), card order, a single-product "feature tour" against multi-product, and catalogue images against designed cards.

:::verify
Specifications such as the number of carousel cards, supported aspect ratios and maximum video lengths differ by placement and change over time. Check the current [Meta Ads Guide](https://www.facebook.com/business/ads-guide/) before you produce creative, rather than relying on figures copied from older articles.
:::

## Matching format to the job

| If the ad needs to… | Usually start with | Consider testing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduce a new brand or product category | Video | A strong static with a single clear claim |
| Demonstrate how something works | Video | A step-by-step carousel |
| Push a time-limited offer | Static | A short video with the offer in the opening seconds |
| Show range or choice | Carousel or catalogue | A collage-style static |
| Re-engage product viewers | Catalogue | A static showing reviews or reassurance |
| Overcome a specific objection | Static or carousel | A short testimonial video |

These are starting points. The point is to choose a format for a reason, then let testing confirm or overturn it.

## How to run a fair format test

Most "video vs static" tests in real accounts are not format tests at all. The video has a different message, a different offer or a different landing page, so the result tells you which *idea* worked, not which format.

A fair test:

1. **Keep the message constant.** Same core claim, same offer, same proof, same landing page. Only the format changes.
2. **Decide the success metric first.** Use the outcome you are paying for, such as cost per purchase, cost per lead or POAS, rather than click-through rate or thumb-stop rate alone. A format that earns cheap clicks can still convert poorly.
3. **Separate the audiences or the delivery.** If all formats sit in one ad set, Meta will quickly favour one of them and the others will get too little delivery to judge. Meta's A/B testing tool or separate, comparable ad sets give each format a fair share.
4. **Give it enough volume.** Decide in advance how many conversions per format you need before reading the result, and do not call a winner on a few days of data.
5. **Repeat with a second concept.** One test tells you about one idea. If the same format wins with two or three different messages, you have learnt something about the format.

:::example A simple format test plan
| | Cell A | Cell B | Cell C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Format | 20-second vertical video | Static product image | Four-card carousel |
| Message | "Sleep cooler: breathable bamboo bedding" | Same | Same, one benefit per card |
| Offer | Free delivery | Same | Same |
| Landing page | Bedding collection | Same | Same |
| Success metric | Cost per purchase | Same | Same |
| Minimum before reading | A conversion threshold agreed in advance | Same | Same |

The figures and product are illustrative. The structure is the point: one variable, one metric, agreed thresholds.
:::

## Benchmarking your creative mix against competitors

Looking at the format mix of competitors' active ads is a fast way to see whether you are out of step with your category. Do it carefully:

- **Use shares, not counts.** A competitor with 80 active ads and one with 8 can only be compared as percentages.
- **Remember counts are not spend.** Meta's Ad Library does not show spend for commercial ads. A brand might have 30 statics live but put most of its budget behind two videos.
- **Look at longevity by format.** If a competitor's videos are mostly new and their statics have been running for months, the statics may be their proven core and the videos their tests, or the other way round.
- **Watch the trend.** A shift from 20% to 50% video over a quarter says more than a single snapshot.
- **Count the unclassified.** Some ads will not have an identifiable format in the data. Leaving them out silently inflates the other shares.

:::impact
### Who this affects

Every Meta advertiser deciding where to spend creative production budget, especially teams being asked to "do more video" without clear evidence.

### What it changes

Treating formats as tools for specific jobs, rather than as a league table, leads to better briefs and to tests that produce usable answers.

### Recommended action

Audit your active ads by format and by the job each ad is doing. Fill gaps where a job has no suitable format, then run one controlled format test where the message, offer and landing page are held constant.

### When no action is needed

If your account already runs a healthy mix of formats, each with clear roles, and your cost per result is stable, there is no need to chase a different mix because of a trend. Benchmark competitors quarterly and move only if the evidence changes.
:::

## Summary

Video, static and carousel are not rivals. They are different tools, and Meta's delivery system works best when it has distinct, well-made options for different people and placements. Choose a format because it suits the message, test it with everything else held constant, judge it on business outcomes, and use competitors' creative mix as context rather than as a target.
